# Climate

Climate change is both a scientific challenge and a practical one. It requires reducing greenhouse-gas emissions, preparing for impacts that are already underway, and communicating evidence in ways that support better decisions.

My work in this sector focuses on making climate information clearer, more relevant and more usable. I translate climate science, Earth-observation data and sustainability research into narratives, communication assets and digital tools that help people understand change and act on it.

## Why climate matters

Climate change affects weather, ecosystems, infrastructure, health, food systems and the places where people live. It is not only a long-term global problem: its effects are already visible through heat, drought, flooding, sea-level rise, changing seasons and pressure on natural systems.

Addressing it involves two connected tasks.

**Mitigation** means reducing or limiting greenhouse-gas emissions, or enhancing the removal and storage of carbon from the atmosphere. **Adaptation** means adjusting human and natural systems to actual or expected climate impacts, reducing vulnerability and building resilience. [258][259]

Neither replaces the other. Emissions reduction limits future change; adaptation helps societies live with the changes already locked in and those still to come.

## My perspective

I approach climate as a communication and translation challenge as much as a technical one.

Climate science can involve complex models, satellite observations, uncertainty ranges, emissions accounting and long time horizons. But its value depends on whether people can connect that evidence to the decisions they face: how a city plans for heat, how a business reduces risk, how a community prepares for flooding, or how policy responds to changing conditions.

My background is in physics, and my work sits at the intersection of climate, sustainability, space and science communication. I am particularly interested in Earth observation and climate services: how satellite and environmental data can make climate impacts more tangible and support more informed action.

## The work I do

My climate work generally falls into four connected areas.

### Climate communication

I develop communication strategies, narratives and content that explain climate science, impacts and responses clearly.

This can include campaign planning, editorial content, video concepts, visual storytelling, event communication and materials for technical, policy and public audiences.

The aim is not simply to repeat the scale of the problem. It is to help audiences understand what is changing, why it matters, what evidence supports it, and what practical responses are available.

### Making climate data meaningful

Climate data becomes most useful when it is connected to a real question: How is temperature changing? What does that mean for heat risk? Where is flooding likely? How can a city, business or community prepare?

I help shape narratives and digital experiences that connect data to context. That includes explaining what a dataset represents, where its limitations lie, and why uncertainty should be communicated honestly rather than hidden.

### Earth observation and climate services

Satellite observations and environmental monitoring provide an important basis for understanding climate systems and supporting climate services.

The Copernicus Climate Change Service combines satellite and in-situ observations with climate models to provide information about past, present and future climate. It offers climate data, monitoring and tools intended to support adaptation and mitigation policy and practice. [253][257][264]

I am interested in how these data sources can be made more accessible, understandable and relevant to the people who need them.

### Sustainability and practical action

Climate work needs to connect evidence to action. That includes emissions reduction, resilience planning, low-carbon transition, environmental management and the practical decisions organisations and communities make.

I help turn technical or policy material into clear communication that supports understanding, engagement and delivery—without overstating certainty or promising simplistic solutions.

## Areas of focus

### Climate science and evidence

Clear climate communication starts with an accurate understanding of the evidence. That means distinguishing between observations, projections and scenarios, and being precise about confidence, uncertainty and limitations.

Climate information is most useful when it explains both the big picture and its local relevance. Global trends matter, but people also need to understand what changing conditions could mean for their own region, sector or community.

### Adaptation and resilience

Adaptation is about preparing for a changing climate: reducing exposure to hazards, strengthening resilience and making better long-term decisions.

This can involve flood risk, heat, drought, coastal change, infrastructure, health, agriculture and emergency planning. Effective adaptation depends not only on technical assessments, but also on clear communication that helps people understand risk and act on it.

### Mitigation and net zero

Mitigation concerns reducing emissions and transitioning towards lower-carbon systems. It spans energy, transport, buildings, industry, land use and consumption.

I am interested in how organisations and public bodies can communicate transition plans, progress and trade-offs honestly. Good mitigation communication avoids vague claims and explains what is actually changing, why, and over what timescale.

### Climate services and Earth observation

Climate services turn data into information that supports decisions. They can combine observations, models, forecasts and projections to help users understand risk and plan for the future.

The Copernicus Climate Change Service provides free and open access to climate data and tools through its Climate Data Store, alongside climate monitoring, seasonal forecasts and climate projections. [253][255][264]

I am particularly interested in how these services can be communicated to audiences beyond specialists, including policymakers, businesses, educators and the public.

## How I work

My approach combines scientific literacy, editorial judgement and practical delivery.

I begin by understanding the audience and the decision they need to make. Then I identify the core message, the evidence behind it and the most useful way to communicate it. Finally, I turn that into formats people can use: articles, videos, visual narratives, websites, event materials or digital tools.

I aim to be accurate about uncertainty, clear about sources and careful not to overstate what data can show. Good climate communication should make complexity easier to engage with—not make it disappear.
