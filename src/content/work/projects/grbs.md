## Overview

Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are the most luminous electromagnetic events in the
Universe since the Big Bang. They occur at cosmological distances, with an
average redshift of around z ≈ 3 and examples beyond z = 6, yet for a few
seconds they can outshine everything else in the gamma-ray sky.

This project was a directed reading (PA4440 Advanced Study Topic) completed
during the fourth year of an MPhys in Physics with Astrophysics at the
University of Leicester, supervised by Dr Richard Willingale. The aim was to
review the theories that attempt to explain the **prompt emission** — the
gamma-rays that arrive first — and to assess how well each stands up against
the observational evidence.

## The problem

The energy involved is extraordinary. Assuming the emission is isotropic, a
GRB can release up to around 10<sup>47</sup> J within a few seconds. For
comparison, the rest-mass energy of the Sun is about 1.8 × 10<sup>47</sup> J
(E = mc²), and a GRB can emit more energy in ten seconds than the Sun will
radiate over its entire ten-billion-year lifetime.

The physical mechanism behind such an outburst is not fully understood. Any
successful model must explain:

- Huge energy output released over very short timescales.
- Two apparent populations: short bursts (roughly under 2 seconds) and long
  bursts (over 2 seconds).
- Variability down to the millisecond scale, implying a very compact source.
- Non-thermal spectra that are well described empirically by the Band function
  but lack a clear theoretical origin.
- A luminosity so high that the radiation must be relativistically beamed, with
  Lorentz factors above 100, to avoid the compactness problem.

## A short history

- **Vela satellites (1960s):** GRBs were discovered by US satellites built to
  detect gamma-ray pulses from nuclear weapons tests in space. The findings
  were published by Klebesadel and colleagues in 1973.
- **CGRO / BATSE (1991–2000):** thousands of bursts showed an isotropic sky
  distribution, casting doubt on a Galactic origin. The V/V<sub>max</sub> test
  and the log N – log P distribution indicated a cosmological population.
- **BeppoSAX (1996):** the first X-ray afterglows allowed accurate positions,
  optical follow-up and redshift measurements, confirming cosmological
  distances and opening the "afterglow era".
- **Swift (2004) and Fermi/GLAST (launched 11 June 2008):** a new generation of
  missions with better localisation and broader energy coverage. A burst
  detected in April 2009 and followed up with ESO's Very Large Telescope was
  measured at a redshift of 8.2, making it the most distant known object at
  the time.

## Generally accepted ingredients

Despite open questions, nearly all current models share several features:

1. **Relativistic motion** with a Lorentz factor Γ > 100, needed to overcome
   the compactness problem. Relativistic beaming means we see only a fraction
   of the source.
2. **Dissipation** of the energy of the relativistic flow, most likely through
   collisionless shocks.
3. **Synchrotron radiation** from relativistic electrons accelerated in those
   shocks. This agrees reasonably well with afterglow observations, but there
   are concerns about whether it fully explains the prompt spectra.
4. **Jets and collimation**, supported by achromatic "jet breaks" in afterglow
   light curves. Beaming reduces the true energy to around 10<sup>51</sup> erg,
   comparable to a supernova.
5. **A compact object** — a black hole or neutron star — as the central
   engine, most likely formed in the same event.

## Possible engines

- **Long bursts:** the collapse of a massive star (a collapsar or hypernova),
  producing a black hole and accretion disc. This is supported by the
  association of long GRBs with core-collapse supernovae and with
  star-forming regions.
- **Short bursts:** the merger of compact binaries such as neutron
  star–neutron star or neutron star–black hole systems. Direct evidence for
  short-burst progenitors was still lacking at the time of this project.

## Physical processes studied

- **Synchrotron radiation:** derivation from the equations of motion of a
  charged particle in a magnetic field, leading to a power-law spectrum for a
  power-law electron distribution.
- **Cooling breaks:** fast-cooling and slow-cooling regimes and the resulting
  broken power-law spectra.
- **Fermi acceleration:** second-order acceleration (energy gain proportional
  to β²) and the more efficient first-order mechanism at shocks (energy gain of
  about 4β/3 per cycle), which naturally produces a power-law particle
  distribution.
- **Synchrotron self-absorption, Compton scattering and inverse Compton
  scattering**, including the limits imposed by the Klein–Nishina regime.
- **Blackbody components:** evidence that a power law plus a black body can fit
  time-resolved BATSE spectra.

## Theories for the prompt emission

### Fireball model (Rees and Mészáros; reviewed by Piran)

A large amount of energy is released into a compact region, forming an opaque
photon–lepton fireball that expands relativistically. In the internal–external
shocks picture:

- **Internal shocks** between shells of ejecta moving at different speeds
  produce the prompt gamma-rays, so the light curve reflects the activity of
  the inner engine.
- **External shocks** between the outflow and the surrounding medium produce
  the longer-lived afterglow.

The model explains the temporal structure well and is the most widely used
framework, but internal shocks convert only a fraction of kinetic energy into
radiation, and a simple synchrotron spectrum does not fit every burst.

### Fireshell model (Ruffini and collaborators)

This model assumes that all GRBs originate from gravitational collapse to a
black hole. An electron–positron plasma created by vacuum polarisation expands
as an optically thick "fireshell". It is described by only two parameters: the
total plasma energy and the baryon loading, B.

The emission splits into a **proper GRB (P-GRB)**, released when the shell
becomes transparent, and an afterglow phase as the baryons interact with the
circumburst medium. Short bursts divide into "genuine" short GRBs (very low
baryon loading, B < 10<sup>-5</sup>) and "fake" short GRBs (a very low-density
environment). The model also connects naturally to the Amati relation between
peak energy and isotropic-equivalent energy (E<sub>p</sub> ∝ E<sub>iso</sub><sup>~0.5</sup>).
It is one of the few attempts to describe the whole GRB phenomenon in one
framework.

### Cannonball model (Dar, and Heinz and Begelman)

The central engine fires a series of narrow, non-spreading "bullets" that are
quickly decelerated by dense circumburst matter. Pulse widths are set by the
slowing-down time, and the burst duration by how long the engine keeps
emitting bullets.

## Assessment

- The afterglow is reasonably well understood; the prompt emission is not.
- Within the internal shocks picture, the temporal structure is reasonably
  understood, but the **origin of the observed spectrum** remains unclear and
  simple synchrotron emission probably needs modifying.
- The link between long GRBs and core-collapse supernovae points clearly to the
  engine of long bursts, but there is no direct or indirect evidence about the
  progenitors of short bursts.
- How the flow is accelerated and collimated, and what sets its variability,
  remain poorly constrained because the inner engine cannot be observed
  directly.
- Open observational questions include the GRB luminosity function, the burst
  rate as a function of redshift, and how closely GRBs trace star formation.

## Teaching outputs

As well as the literature review, the project required the design of teaching
materials.

### Six-lecture course outline

1. Introduction, history, energetics, distribution and the short/long split.
2. Observational constraints, prompt emission and light curves.
3. Non-thermal spectra, power laws, synchrotron radiation, Fermi
   acceleration, cooling breaks and inverse Compton scattering.
4. The fireball model: compactness problem, internal shocks, efficiency and
   Sedov analysis.
5. The fireshell and cannonball models, the Amati relation and the B parameter.
6. Future prospects, models and missions, including GLAST/Fermi.

### 40-minute lecture

Delivered on 14 May 2009, covering the history of GRB observations, their
observed properties, the generally accepted ingredients, the main prompt
emission models and their physical processes, and open questions for future
research.

### Suggested problems

1. **Energy calculation:** estimate the energy needed to "evaporate" the Sun,
   convert fluence to isotropic energy at the mean GRB redshift, and compare
   with the Eddington luminosity.
2. **Source distribution:** show that a homogeneous distribution gives a
   log N – log F slope of −3/2.
3. **Synchrotron radiation:** derive the equations of motion, the cyclotron
   frequency and the power-law spectrum from a power-law electron distribution.
4. **Fermi acceleration:** derive the mean energy gain for second-order and
   first-order acceleration and the resulting energy after n collisions.
