## Overview

The Queen Elizabeth Diamond Jubilee Wood (QEDJW) is a large, young woodland
and wetland site in the National Forest, in north-west Leicestershire, close to
the village of Normanton le Heath. It was planted to mark the Queen's Diamond
Jubilee in 2012 and is the flagship site of the Woodland Trust's Jubilee Woods
project.

Since July 2024 I have volunteered here with the Woodland Trust, supporting
the practical work of caring for the wood and the experience of the people and
wildlife that use it.

## The wood at a glance

- **Location:** Normanton le Heath, in the National Forest, north-west
  Leicestershire (grid reference SK387122).
- **Distance:** about 24.1 km (15 miles) from Leicester, 6.4 km (4 miles) from
  Ashby-de-la-Zouch and 11.2 km (7 miles) from Coalville.
- **Size:** 116.38 hectares (287.57 acres).
- **Managed by:** the Woodland Trust.
- **Features:** on-site parking, public access, grassland, a lake, a bird
  hide, a pond-dipping platform and a large earth artwork.

## Landscape and habitats

QEDJW is more than a plantation. The site combines several habitats that
support one another:

- **New native woodland:** around 300,000 native broadleaf trees were planted
  between 2012 and 2015, including oak, field maple, silver birch, beech and
  hornbeam. The wood is now maturing into a beautiful young woodland.
- **Ancient woodland areas:** at the far end of the site, where bluebells and
  wood anemones carpet the ground in spring.
- **Wetland and lake:** home to vegetation such as bulrush and yellow iris,
  and an important draw for water birds.
- **Open grassland:** valuable for ground-nesting birds, butterflies and
  hares.

This mix of semi-natural ancient woodland, new planting and wetland makes the
site a good example of woodland creation working at landscape scale alongside
the wider National Forest.

## Wildlife

The wood is especially rewarding for birdwatchers:

- **Open ground:** skylarks and other ground-nesting birds.
- **The lake:** mute swans, little grebes, tufted ducks, terns and mallards.
- **Raptors:** buzzards and, rarely, the hen harrier.
- **At night:** tawny and barn owls hunting over the grassland.

In spring and summer the site is also good for butterflies, including the
striking marbled white. Deer and brown hares can be seen, and typical woodland
residents such as foxes, badgers, stoats and weasels share the site with
woodpeckers, bullfinches and tits.

## Art in the landscape

A striking feature of the wood is *Now & Then*, an earth artwork by artist
Rosie Leventon. It takes the form of a giant spiral descending into the ground,
and visitors can walk down the corkscrew. Oak and wild service trees have been
planted on the structure, so that over time it will become a shady, private
space within the wood. Along with the willow sculpture at the bird hide, the
artwork makes the site distinctive and popular with families.

## Visiting and access

- **Parking:** a signed car park on Heather Lane with space for 53 cars. It is
  locked overnight, opening at 8am and closing at 8pm from April to October and
  5pm from November to March.
- **Paths:** three waymarked, surfaced circular trails run from the car park to
  the lake. The main loop is suitable for wheelchairs and pushchairs. A longer
  white route of about 3 km links the 61 Diamond Jubilee Groves, passes the
  lake and continues to the ancient woodland.
- **Gates:** pedestrian gates are an all-abilities design with a RADAR key
  padlock; others are standard kissing gates.
- **Terrain:** a number of long but gentle slopes on first entering the wood.
- **Access advice:** use the Heather Lane entrance rather than School Lane in
  Normanton le Heath, which is narrow.
- **Public transport:** there are no buses to Normanton le Heath itself, though
  services run from Leicester to Coalville and from Coalville to Ravenstone.
  The nearest railway stations are Burton, Tamworth and Loughborough, each
  around 24 to 26 km away.
- **Facilities:** there are no toilets on site; the nearest are in Coalville
  and Ashby.

*Opening hours, access arrangements and facilities can change, so check the
Woodland Trust website before visiting.*

## Things to do

- **Walking:** follow the trails to discover the artwork, groves, lake and
  ancient woodland.
- **Birdwatching:** the bird hide, funded by the Arts Council, sits just south
  of the lake and includes a willow sculpture.
- **Pond-dipping:** a purpose-built platform on the western edge of the lake
  lets families catch and release minibeasts.
- **Dogs:** welcome, following the Woodland Trust's guidance for walking dogs
  in its woods.
- **Dedications:** trees, benches or areas of woodland can be dedicated at the
  site.

## History

Archaeological finds on the site stretch back to the Neolithic period. They
include flint shards and medieval pottery, and a Bronze Age palstave (axe) in
mint condition found by a farm labourer in the 1970s. The land later became
part of the Jubilee Woods project and the National Forest's transformation of
former mining and farmland into woodland, with nearby extension sites (QEDJW
Extension and QEDJW Ext 2) adding to the wider woodland network.

## Why it matters

Young woodland creation sites depend on continued care as trees establish and
habitats mature. Volunteers help to keep paths safe, monitor wildlife and
support a welcoming experience, so that a site created for a royal jubilee
remains a lasting public benefit: a place for nature recovery, climate
resilience, learning and wellbeing within easy reach of Leicester.
