# Food Security and Resilience Copernicus Hackathon

## Feed the World, Invent the Future

The **Food Security and Resilience Copernicus Hackathon** was a two-day collaborative innovation event held at the **University of Leicester on 19–20 September 2019**.

Bringing together coders, researchers, scientists, designers, storytellers, entrepreneurs and problem-solvers, the hackathon challenged participants to use **Copernicus Earth observation data and services** to develop practical solutions to challenges surrounding food security, water security and resilience in a changing climate.

Over an intensive **48 hours**, multidisciplinary teams developed prototypes, explored new applications for satellite data and investigated opportunities for collaboration between academia, industry and the wider community.

## About the Hackathon

The event was built around a simple challenge:

> **Feed the World, Invent the Future**

Food security requires people to have access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food while ensuring that food systems remain sustainable into the future.

Achieving this requires better understanding of the environmental, economic and societal pressures affecting food production and consumption.

The hackathon provided a practical environment in which participants could explore how Earth observation and space-enabled technologies could contribute to addressing these challenges.

## Copernicus and Earth Observation

The hackathon centred on the **Copernicus programme**, the European Union's Earth observation programme.

Copernicus provides access to extensive environmental data and services derived from satellites, in-situ observations and other sources.

Participants were encouraged to explore how this open data could be transformed into useful applications and services.

Potential applications included:

- Agriculture
- Food production
- Water security
- Environmental monitoring
- Climate resilience
- Logistics
- Supply chains
- Resource mapping
- Risk assessment
- Insurance
- Smart cities
- Decision support

The emphasis was not simply on analysing satellite data, but on turning Earth observation into practical solutions.

## Challenge Themes

Participants could select their own challenge within the broad theme of food and water security and resilience.

Suggested areas included:

### Smart Agriculture

Using Earth observation and other technologies to improve agricultural decision-making, productivity and resilience.

### Logistics and Supply Chains

Exploring how satellite data, logistics technologies and emerging digital infrastructure could improve food supply chains.

### Blockchain and Food Security

Investigating potential applications of distributed technologies in food and water supply chains, provenance and resilience.

### Smart Insurance

Developing new services that could use Earth observation data to understand environmental risks and support innovative insurance products.

### Resource Mapping

Using satellite observations to map, monitor and understand environmental and agricultural resources.

### Societal and Technical Challenges

Participants were also encouraged to identify their own challenges relevant to food security, water security and resilience.

## The 48-Hour Challenge

The core hackathon took place over **19 and 20 September 2019**.

Teams worked intensively over two days to move from an initial problem or idea towards a practical prototype.

The process encouraged rapid experimentation and multidisciplinary collaboration.

Participants combined:

- Copernicus data
- Coding
- Data analysis
- Design
- Research
- Business modelling
- Storytelling
- Domain expertise

The objective was to produce something tangible rather than simply discuss potential applications.

## Rapid MVP Development

Participants received preparatory training before the main event.

A training session on **12 September 2019** introduced selected teams and other participants to:

- Agile development
- Rapid MVP development
- Copernicus data access
- Relevant coding libraries
- Rapid prototyping approaches

This provided teams with a common foundation before the 48-hour event.

The focus on **minimum viable products (MVPs)** helped participants move rapidly from ideas towards demonstrable concepts.

## Knowledge Exchange

The hackathon was designed as a knowledge-exchange activity rather than a conventional coding competition.

It brought together people with different skills and perspectives, allowing technical expertise to be combined with knowledge of food systems, Earth observation, business and societal challenges.

This multidisciplinary approach was central to the event.

A successful solution could require expertise from several different domains, for example:

**Satellite data + agriculture + software + business + user needs**

rather than technology development in isolation.

## From Data to Impact

A central theme of the hackathon was the transformation of raw Earth observation data into useful information and ultimately actionable solutions.

The process can be represented as:

**Earth observation data → Information → Insight → Application → Impact**

Copernicus provides the data infrastructure, but the value emerges when that data is combined with domain knowledge, software, business models and an understanding of user needs.

The hackathon provided a practical environment for exploring this transition.

## Food Security and Resilience

Food security is not simply a question of producing more food.

It is influenced by a complex combination of:

- Climate
- Water availability
- Land use
- Agricultural productivity
- Supply chains
- Infrastructure
- Markets
- Environmental change
- Resource availability
- Human behaviour

Earth observation can provide a powerful perspective because satellites can repeatedly observe large areas of the planet.

This creates opportunities to monitor change, identify risks and support decisions across different scales.

## Why the Hackathon Matters

The Food Security and Resilience Copernicus Hackathon demonstrated a model for bringing together **space data, multidisciplinary expertise and rapid innovation** around a real-world sustainability challenge.

Rather than treating Earth observation as an end in itself, the event focused on the question:

> **What can we build with this data that makes a difference?**

That approach remains relevant as Earth observation becomes increasingly important to climate adaptation, sustainable agriculture, resilience and environmental decision-making.

## Legacy

The hackathon formed part of the development of Leicester as a centre for **space-enabled innovation and Earth observation applications**.

It brought together an unusually broad ecosystem spanning:

**University research → Space sector → Earth observation → Food systems → Government → Industry → Students → Entrepreneurs**

The event also provided an example of how relatively short, focused innovation programmes can create connections between communities that do not traditionally work together.
