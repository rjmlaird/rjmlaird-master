## Overview

SEPPCoN, the **Survey of the Ensemble Physical Properties of Cometary Nuclei**,
was a collaborative programme investigating the physical characteristics of
Jupiter-family comet nuclei.

Jupiter-family comets are short-period comets whose orbits are strongly shaped
by Jupiter. Their nuclei are relatively primitive Solar System bodies, making
them useful targets for studying the composition, structure and evolutionary
history of material left from planet formation.

The programme combined thermal-infrared measurements from the **Spitzer Space
Telescope** with visible-light observations from ground-based telescopes. This
multi-wavelength approach was used to constrain nucleus sizes, surface
reflectivity and thermal behaviour for a large sample of cometary nuclei.

## Research objectives

The project aimed to improve population-level understanding of Jupiter-family
comets by addressing several connected questions:

- What are the typical sizes and geometric albedos of cometary nuclei?
- Is there evidence that albedo varies systematically with nucleus size?
- What do thermal measurements reveal about the surface properties of nuclei?
- How does the size distribution of observed nuclei inform models of cometary
  evolution and the small-body populations of the Solar System?
- Can thermal and visible-light observations be combined to reduce uncertainty
  in estimates of nucleus size and reflectivity?

## Observations

### Spitzer Space Telescope

Spitzer observations measured thermal emission from cometary nuclei at
mid-infrared wavelengths. The programme used imaging capabilities associated
with the Infrared Spectrograph (IRS) and the Multiband Imaging Photometer for
Spitzer (MIPS), including observations around 16 and 22 micrometres.

Thermal-infrared observations are particularly valuable because they provide a
constraint on the heat emitted by a body. When combined with visible-light
measurements, they help break the degeneracy between an object's physical size
and its surface reflectivity.

A small, bright object and a larger, dark object can have similar brightness in
visible light. Thermal emission provides additional information that enables
more reliable estimates of effective radius and geometric albedo.

### Ground-based optical observations

Ground-based visible-light observations complemented the Spitzer measurements.
They provided photometry and contextual observations needed to interpret the
thermal signal and separate nucleus properties from the effects of cometary
activity, dust and observing geometry.

Together, the space- and ground-based observations supported a more robust
physical characterisation of individual comets and the wider Jupiter-family
comet population.

## Methods

The analysis combined thermal-infrared fluxes with optical photometry to derive
physical parameters for cometary nuclei.

Key steps included:

- Measuring thermal emission at mid-infrared wavelengths.
- Using visible-light observations to constrain reflected sunlight.
- Applying thermal models to estimate effective nucleus radii.
- Calculating geometric albedos from combined thermal and optical constraints.
- Comparing derived properties across the survey sample.
- Examining the cumulative size distribution of Jupiter-family comet nuclei.

The programme also considered thermal inertia, a property describing how quickly
a material responds to heating and cooling. Low thermal inertia is consistent
with a porous, dusty and weakly conducting surface layer.

## Findings

SEPPCoN results indicated that many Jupiter-family comet nuclei have low thermal
inertia, consistent with surfaces that are porous, dusty and relatively poor
conductors of heat.

Preliminary analysis of the cumulative size distribution found a power-law slope
similar to results from visible-light studies. This suggested that there was no
strong evidence for a systematic relationship between nucleus size and albedo
across the observed sample.

The survey provided a larger and more consistently analysed dataset for
evaluating the physical properties of Jupiter-family comet nuclei than was
available from isolated observations alone.

## Scientific context

Cometary nuclei preserve material from the early Solar System and may retain
information about the environments in which planetesimals formed. Understanding
their size distribution, albedo and thermal properties helps connect present-day
comet observations with models of Solar System evolution.

Jupiter-family comets are especially important because their orbits bring them
into the inner Solar System, where their nuclei can be studied in detail.
However, activity, dust and observational selection effects can complicate
physical interpretation. Combining thermal-infrared and visible-light
measurements provides a stronger basis for estimating intrinsic nucleus
properties.

## Limitations

Cometary nuclei are difficult targets because activity can obscure or alter the
signal from the solid nucleus. Thermal models also depend on assumptions about
surface roughness, emissivity, rotation, observing geometry and thermal
properties.

The project therefore provides population-level constraints rather than a
complete physical description of every observed comet. Derived radii and
albedos should be interpreted alongside model assumptions and the quality of
the available optical and thermal measurements.
