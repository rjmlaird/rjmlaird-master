## Overview

**Vortex** is a wind-resource and site-assessment service that uses
meteorological modelling and climate data to support renewable-energy project
development.

This project communicated how Vortex uses information from the **Copernicus
Climate Change Service** (C3S), implemented by the European Centre for
Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), to provide wind information for
planning and safeguarding wind-energy projects.

I contributed editorial and science-communication work through Design & Data,
helping turn a technical climate-services use case into a clear story about the
practical value of climate data for renewable-energy decisions.

## Context

Wind-energy projects depend on reliable estimates of wind conditions over long
periods. Developers need to understand expected energy yield, local terrain
effects, seasonal variation, extreme conditions and the uncertainty associated
with a proposed site.

Direct wind measurements are valuable, but they can be limited in geographic
coverage and historical duration. Climate reanalysis datasets provide a
consistent, long-term record that can be combined with local modelling to
support site selection, energy-yield estimates and project risk assessment.

Vortex provides online “Wind & Site” information derived from mesoscale
meteorological modelling and ERA5 climate reanalysis data produced by ECMWF.
[web:231]

## The challenge

The project needed to communicate a complex technical chain in practical terms:

Global climate reanalysis
→ high-resolution meteorological modelling
→ site-specific wind information
→ renewable-energy development decisions

This meant explaining why climate data matters to audiences beyond meteorology
and Earth observation, including wind-farm developers, investors, engineers,
planners and policy stakeholders.

The editorial challenge was to make the connection between climate data and
energy infrastructure concrete:

- How can a long-term climate record help estimate wind resources?
- Why do local terrain and atmospheric conditions matter?
- What does downscaling add beyond coarse global information?
- How can climate services reduce uncertainty during project development?
- How can data support more resilient renewable-energy investment?

## Vortex and C3S

Vortex uses C3S data for its online wind-modelling services.

The Copernicus Climate Change Service provides climate information based on
observations, models and reanalysis. ECMWF operates C3S on behalf of the
European Union and produces the ERA5 global climate reanalysis dataset used in
many climate-service applications. [web:231]

Vortex combines large-scale climate information with mesoscale modelling to
produce wind-resource data at a more useful spatial scale for energy projects.
Its services are designed to support planning and risk management across the
renewable-energy project lifecycle. [web:231]

## My role

I worked as an **Editor and science communicator** through Design & Data.

My contribution supported the editorial development of an ECMWF and Copernicus
Climate Change Service user story, explaining how Vortex applied climate data
to a commercial and operational renewable-energy context.

The work included:

- Translating climate-service concepts into accessible language.
- Developing a clear narrative around renewable-energy decision-making.
- Explaining the relationship between reanalysis, modelling and wind-resource
  assessment.
- Supporting video and multimedia communication.
- Connecting technical data infrastructure with its social, commercial and
  environmental value.

## Editorial approach

The central editorial idea was simple:

> Climate data helps turn uncertainty about future energy resources into better
> evidence for renewable-energy decisions.

Rather than presenting ERA5 or mesoscale modelling as isolated technical
products, the story followed the route from data to outcome.

```text
Climate record
→ Wind conditions
→ Site assessment
→ Energy-yield estimate
→ Project planning and investment
```

This approach gave the viewer a practical reason to care about climate
services: they help developers make more informed choices when designing
renewable-energy infrastructure.

## Wind-resource modelling

Wind conditions vary across time and geography. A site can be affected by
large-scale weather patterns, seasonal shifts, land cover, elevation, terrain,
surface roughness and local flow effects.

Vortex uses numerical weather modelling to translate broader atmospheric
conditions into higher-resolution wind information. Its modelling workflow
uses Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF)-based approaches and
high-performance computing to downscale atmospheric data for wind-resource
applications. [web:233]

The resulting information can be used where direct measurements are limited,
while still requiring appropriate validation, local expertise and careful
interpretation in project-specific decisions.

## Outcomes

The completed case study and video supported public and professional

Key outcomes included:

- A practical example of climate data contributing to renewable-energy

  systems and investment decisions.

## Limitations

This project concerned editorial communication and climate-service storytelling.
It did not involve developing the Vortex platform, producing ERA5 itself or
operating the underlying wind models.

Modelled wind-resource information is not a substitute for all on-site
measurement and engineering assessment. Results depend on data quality, model
configuration, local terrain, observation availability and the specific
requirements of a proposed energy project.
